Instructor guide · L01 · Units 1.1–1.4

Business Analytics Introduction

A practical speaking structure for the two-hour theory deck, followed by a two-hour Jamovi lab using the UCI Bank Marketing dataset.

Theory: 120 min Lab: 120 min Primary dataset: UCI Bank Marketing Software: Jamovi
Before class

Instructor setup

  • Install Jamovi on the instructor machine and test opening the semicolon-delimited CSV.
  • Download and extract bank-additional-full.csv from the UCI Bank Marketing archive.
  • Prepare a backup copy in the LMS or shared folder; downloading 40,000 rows live can fail.
  • Have students create a folder named ITMA501-L01-Jamovi and save an .omv file there.
  • Keep the lab question visible: Which customer characteristics are associated with a positive campaign response?
Slide 1 · Title3–5 min

Open the course with the decision, not the definitions

Welcome students and frame analytics as a way to improve decisions. Explain that the class will move from a business question to evidence, and that the lab will make the idea concrete.

Ask: “What is one decision in your work or organization that should be more data-informed?” Take two or three answers and reuse one as a running example.

Set expectations: this is not a software tour. The tools matter because they let students inspect evidence and defend a conclusion.

Slide 2 · Agenda5 min

Give students the map

Walk across the four units in order. Tell students that each concept answers a different question:

  1. What kind of answer are we seeking?
  2. What makes a dataset “big” or difficult?
  3. What shape does the data have?
  4. Where do organizations use these ideas?

Explain the rhythm: theory supplies vocabulary; the lab lets students use that vocabulary on a real business dataset.

Slide 3 · Unit 1.125 min

Methods and model families

Use one retail example throughout. Begin with a monthly sales table and progressively change the question. Emphasize that the four families are not competing labels; they are increasingly decision-oriented uses of evidence.

Descriptive

“What happened?” Show totals, averages, counts, and distributions.

Diagnostic

“Why did it happen?” Compare segments, time periods, or operational causes.

Predictive

“What is likely to happen?” Estimate a future value or probability.

Prescriptive

“What should we do?” Select an action under constraints.

Suggested walkthrough with Bank Marketing

  1. Start with y and show a frequency table: subscribed versus did not subscribe.
  2. Ask what might explain the difference. Introduce contact, job, and education as possible comparison variables.
  3. Use a crosstab to compare response rates by one variable. Name the denominator: “Among customers contacted by cellular phone, X% subscribed.”
  4. Explain that a predictive model could estimate y for a new customer using age, job, education, and contact method.
  5. Finish with the prescriptive decision: whom should the bank contact, through which channel, and how often?

Jamovi demonstration: establish the outcome first

  1. Open Analyses → Exploration → Descriptives.
  2. Move y into the analysis field.
  3. Request a frequency table and bar chart.
  4. Ask students: “What proportion of contacted customers subscribed?”
  5. Explain that y = yes means the customer subscribed to a term deposit and y = no means they did not.
  6. Reuse y as the outcome when comparing contact, job, or education with crosstabs.
Boundary: Jamovi supports the descriptive and diagnostic demonstration today. Treat predictive and prescriptive analytics as the next decision layer, not as conclusions from this simple comparison.
Check understanding: give a scenario such as “customers who may cancel next month.” Ask students to identify the model family and explain why.
Do not overpromise: predictive and prescriptive analytics do not automatically produce correct decisions. Data quality, assumptions, and costs still matter.
Slide 4 · Unit 1.225 min

The 4 V’s are operational questions

Present each V as a constraint that changes how an analyst works. Avoid treating “Big Data” as only a very large file.

VConvey it asClass prompt
VolumeHow much must we store or process?Could Jamovi hold this dataset?
VelocityHow quickly does information arrive?Would a weekly report be enough?
VarietyHow many formats and schemas must we combine?What changes when we add JSON or text?
VeracityHow much should we trust the measurement?What could make a “yes” response misleading?

Connect forward to the lab: the UCI file is manageable in volume, mostly structured, and still requires veracity checks such as missing values and ambiguous categories.

Suggested walkthrough with the lab dataset

  1. Volume: show that roughly 40,000 rows are substantial enough to feel real but small enough for Jamovi.
  2. Velocity: point out that the file is historical and static; it represents batch analysis, not live campaign monitoring.
  3. Variety: identify numeric fields such as age and categorical fields such as job and contact; explain that multiple types coexist in one table.
  4. Veracity: inspect “unknown” values, the meaning of pdays = 999, and the fact that duration is only known after a call ends.
  5. Ask students which V would become the main problem if the bank received millions of new call events every day.
Slide 5 · Unit 1.320 min

Data shape determines the first tool

Contrast three representations of the same business event: a row in a table, a nested JSON event, and a customer email. Ask students what can be counted immediately and what requires transformation.

  • Structured: fixed rows and columns; ideal for Jamovi, SQL, spreadsheets.
  • Semi-structured: nested or optional fields; requires parsing and normalization.
  • Unstructured: text, images, audio, or video; requires extraction before conventional analysis.

Suggested walkthrough with three representations

  1. Show one Bank Marketing row as a structured record: one customer/contact event with named columns.
  2. Imagine the same event arriving as JSON, with nested campaign metadata and optional fields. Ask what must be extracted before Jamovi can use it.
  3. Imagine a call transcript or customer email. Explain that the text is useful, but must first be converted into features such as sentiment or topic.
  4. Return to the CSV and explicitly connect the tool choice: Jamovi is appropriate because the immediate questions concern rows, columns, counts, and distributions.
Bridge: “Today we will stay with structured data so we can focus on analytical reasoning rather than file engineering.”
Slide 6 · Unit 1.420 min

Make the domains feel like decisions

For each domain, name the decision first and the technique second. For example: “How much inventory should we hold?” leads to forecasting and optimization in supply chain.

Supply chain

Forecast demand, reduce stockouts, optimize inventory.

Finance

Detect fraud, assess risk, prioritize investigations.

Marketing

Segment customers, evaluate campaigns, improve response.

HR and Web

Understand attrition, personalize experiences, improve conversion.

Use the Bank Marketing dataset as the transition: it is a marketing dataset, but students can practice all four model questions around it.

Suggested domain connections

  • Supply chain: translate the same logic to “which products will stock out?”
  • Finance: translate it to “which transaction deserves review?” and distinguish a risk score from a final decision.
  • Marketing: use y as the response outcome and discuss campaign targeting.
  • HR: compare the idea with attrition prediction, emphasizing privacy and fairness.
  • Web: compare campaign response with conversion or recommendation behavior.
Teaching move: have students choose one domain and state one descriptive, one predictive, and one prescriptive question for it.
Slide 7 · Dataset options10 min

Choose a dataset that teaches the method

Recommend UCI Bank Marketing for this class. It has a clear business outcome (y), numeric variables such as age and duration, categorical variables such as job and education, and enough rows to feel real without overwhelming Jamovi.

  • Primary: UCI Bank Marketing — best fit for the planned lab.
  • Fallback: Titanic — small and familiar, but less directly connected to business decisions.
  • Alternative: Gapminder — excellent for indicators and plots, weaker for a single response outcome.

Explain that the dataset is not proof of causation. Students are looking for patterns and associations, not claiming that a variable causes a campaign response.

Recommended choice and download plan

  1. Use UCI Bank Marketing as the common class dataset so every group can follow the same demonstration.
  2. Download the ZIP before class and keep a copy in the LMS. The archive contains bank-additional-full.csv, which uses a semicolon delimiter.
  3. Have students open the file in Jamovi and confirm that the first row is variable names and that categorical values are not being treated as numbers.
  4. Use Titanic only as a backup if the UCI download or extraction fails; it is simpler, but less aligned with the marketing decision in this lecture.
Slide 8 · Summary10 min

Close theory by making students say the framework

Ask students to complete this sentence aloud: “A good analyst matches the business question to a model, evaluates the data’s 4 V’s, identifies its shape, and communicates a decision-relevant finding.”

Transition to lab: “Now we will test the framework. We will download one dataset, inspect its structure and trustworthiness, then produce evidence for one marketing decision.”

Lab · Instructor walkthrough

How to guide students without taking over

  1. Open: Jamovi → Open → This PC/Mac → select the CSV. If prompted, confirm semicolon delimiter and that the first row contains variable names.
  2. Set measurement levels: nominal for categories such as job and y; continuous for age, duration, and campaign. Explain that type selection changes available analyses.
  3. Inspect before analyzing: ask students to identify “unknown” values, zeros, extreme durations, and variables whose meaning is not obvious. Do not silently delete unusual rows.
  4. Frequencies: Analyses → Exploration → Descriptives. Move categorical variables into the analysis and request frequency tables and bar plots.
  5. Numeric descriptives: add mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and histogram. Ask whether the mean is representative when the distribution is skewed.
  6. Crosstabs: Frequencies → Contingency Tables → Independent Samples. Put y in rows and a candidate variable in columns; request counts and row percentages.
  7. Interpret: require students to state the denominator: “Among customers contacted by cellular phone, X% subscribed.” This prevents confusing counts with rates.
  8. Save: File → Save As → UAGM-ITMA501-L01-Group##.omv. Export key tables or plots as images/PDF only after the analysis is saved.
Teaching guardrail: do not let students rank variables only by the largest count. The useful comparison is usually a response percentage within each group.
Lab · Required deliverable

What students should produce

  1. A saved Jamovi .omv file with the original data, correctly typed variables, and reproducible analyses.
  2. One data-quality note: missingness, ambiguous values, sampling limitation, or a measurement concern.
  3. One frequency table and chart for a categorical variable.
  4. One descriptive table and histogram for a numeric variable.
  5. One crosstab with counts and row percentages.
  6. A 200–250 word interpretation answering: “Which three features appear most associated with a positive campaign response, and what should we avoid claiming?”

Grade the reasoning, not whether the group discovers a particular “winning” variable. A careful limitation is evidence of learning.

Close · Debrief

End with evidence, uncertainty, and next steps

Have each group report in this order: question → method → result → limitation → action or next question. This gives students a repeatable communication pattern for the rest of the course.

Final bridge: “Today we inspected a flat file. In L02, we will learn how relational databases store the same kind of information across connected tables and how SQL retrieves it.”
